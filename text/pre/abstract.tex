\begin{resumo}[ABSTRACT]
\begin{otherlanguage*}{english}\SingleSpacing
This study examines how the economic literature's diagnosis of the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on the labor market has changed with the rise of generative AI and to what extent that change follows the technological object under study. It conducts a systematic review, guided by the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, of studies with publication dates from 2013 onward indexed in Web of Science and Scopus as of May 16, 2026, the search date. Out of 3,118 records, 816 studies were included and coded by language models, without human verification of a sample and, in 86\% of cases, from the abstract alone, by type of evidence, technology in focus, the mechanisms of the Acemoglu--Restrepo task framework, the sign of the employment effect, and the skill group affected. A study is taken to locate a group at risk when it identifies that group as affected and does not attribute a positive sign to the employment effect. Among the 523 studies (64\%) that classify the affected group, the share locating the risk in high-skill occupations rises from 2.0\% (3 of 147) among those published up to 2022 to 8.5\% (32 of 376) among those published from 2023 onward, although risk for low-skill workers remains the most frequent diagnosis. The change is associated mainly with the technological object: high-skill risk is the most frequent category among generative-AI studies that classify the affected group (15 of 33; 45.5\%, 95\% confidence interval 29.8\% to 62.0\%) and is residual among studies of robotics and automation, which serve as a comparison group. Once the object is controlled for, the effect of the publication period cannot be distinguished from zero, although the number of studies does not allow ruling out a residual calendar effect, nor locating a break at the launch of ChatGPT. The result is consistent with the task approach, under which risk follows the content of the tasks each technology performs. Other changes, such as shorter horizons and a more frequently positive sign, also occur within the robotics literature and are general trends of the field. The sign attributed to the employment effect is associated with the type of evidence (Cramér's $V$ of 0.30): occupational exposure studies lean negative, firm-level studies are the most likely to report positive effects, and theoretical models and literature reviews lean ambiguous. Limitations regarding search coverage and the use of language models as extractors are discussed.

\noindent
\textbf{Keywords}: artificial intelligence; generative AI; labor market; employment; task approach; systematic literature review.
\end{otherlanguage*}
\end{resumo}
